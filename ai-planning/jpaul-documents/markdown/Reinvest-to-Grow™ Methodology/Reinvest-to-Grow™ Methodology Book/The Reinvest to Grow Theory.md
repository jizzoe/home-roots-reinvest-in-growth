Preface

During nearly a decade working with women entrepreneurs in Haiti, Home Roots Foundation observed a recurring pattern. Many businesses increased their income after receiving training and financing, yet relatively few achieved sustained enterprise transformation. Working capital was repeatedly depleted by household shocks, inventory was purchased at retail prices, and reinvestment was inconsistent. These observations led to a fundamental question: Why do some enterprises continue growing while others plateau despite similar increases in income?

This book is the result of that question.

**Executive Summary**

Despite decades of investment in microfinance, entrepreneurship training, savings programs, and business development services, many microenterprises remain trapped in subsistence-level activity. While these interventions frequently increase business income, relatively few enterprises accumulate sufficient productive assets to achieve sustained growth, resilience, or graduation into small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs).

The Reinvest-to-Grow™ Theory proposes that this outcome reflects a fundamental distinction between **income generation** and **enterprise transformation**.

The theory argues that increases in income alone do not necessarily produce lasting business growth. Instead, enterprise transformation depends on an entrepreneur's ability to continuously accumulate productive capital—such as working capital, inventory, equipment, technology, knowledge, and business capabilities—at a rate that exceeds consumption demands and economic shocks.

Accordingly, enterprise development should be evaluated not only by changes in income, but also by improvements in productive capital accumulation, enterprise capability, resilience, and long-term business growth.

To organize these ideas, the Reinvest-to-Grow™ Framework identifies five interacting mechanisms of enterprise transformation:

- **Productive Capital Accumulation** — increasing the stock of assets that expand future productive capacity.
- **Enterprise Capability** — improving the entrepreneur's ability to allocate capital effectively.
- **Behavioral Reinvestment** — encouraging consistent reinvestment of retained earnings into productive uses.
- **Enterprise Ecosystems** — strengthening the institutional environment that supports enterprise growth.
- **Adaptive Learning** — continuously improving interventions through evidence and feedback.

The Reinvest-to-Grow™ Methodology translates these principles into practice through an integrated enterprise growth system. Rather than delivering financing, training, or market access independently, the methodology combines:

- collective purchasing to reduce inventory costs and increase business margins,
- graduated growth capital that finances productive investment,
- enterprise growth services that strengthen business capability,
- an Enterprise Growth Platform that coordinates implementation and data collection,
- continuous measurement that enables learning and adaptation.

The Enterprise Supply Hub serves as the first implementation component of this methodology. It functions simultaneously as a revenue-generating business, a service delivery center, and a research environment for validating the theory's assumptions before broader digital expansion.

Unlike many enterprise development models, Reinvest-to-Grow™ is explicitly designed as a scientific framework. It generates testable hypotheses, encourages independent evaluation, and anticipates continuous refinement as new evidence emerges.

The theory does not claim to replace existing enterprise development approaches. Rather, it seeks to integrate insights from microfinance, behavioral economics, institutional economics, entrepreneurship research, market systems development, and complexity science into a coherent explanation of enterprise growth centered on productive capital accumulation.

Its ultimate contribution is therefore not simply a new methodology, but a broader proposition: that enterprise development can evolve into an evidence-based learning science capable of continuously improving its understanding of how entrepreneurs build resilient businesses and how institutions can most effectively support that process.
